## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Femur model

A single right femur was used. Its periosteal and endosteal surfaces were available as two closed-solid STEP files (cortical shell, 287 cm³; cancellous core, 197 cm³) derived from a CT segmentation. The cortical solid was hollow, i.e. its inner shell coincided with the cancellous solid, so the two solids partitioned the bone without overlap. The STEP geometry was imported through the OpenCASCADE kernel of gmsh 4.15.2 and tessellated at 0.8 mm for landmark detection and distance-field construction; the same solids were used for finite-element (FE) meshing, so the geometry seen by the templating step and by the FE model was identical.

Anatomical landmarks were detected automatically on the periosteal surface: the femoral head centre and radius by iterative sphere fitting (25.3 mm), the shaft axis by a least-squares line through the centroids of transverse periosteal sections 150–330 mm below the head, the posterior condylar line from the most posterior points of the medial and lateral condyles, the neck axis from the head centre through the centroids of successive neck sections, and the lesser trochanter from the postero-medial prominence of the periosteal contour. A femur-based coordinate system was defined with z along the shaft axis (proximal positive), x in the transverse plane along the posterior condylar line (medial positive) and y = z × x (posterior positive); the origin was placed on the shaft axis at the level of the lesser trochanter. The neck–shaft angle was 137.5°, the anteversion 6.1° and the femoral offset 39.5 mm. The canal flare index (canal width 20 mm above the lesser trochanter divided by the isthmus width) was 5.4 and the cortical index at 100 mm below the lesser trochanter was 0.68, i.e. a Dorr type A femur.

Signed distance fields to the endosteal and periosteal surfaces were computed on a 0.5 mm voxel grid (ray-parity inside test, Euclidean distance transform, exact point-to-triangle refinement within 3 mm of the surface). The volume enclosed by each field agreed with the OpenCASCADE volume of the corresponding solid to within 0.1 %, and the 95th percentile of the absolute field value sampled on the surfaces was 0.06 mm.

### 2.2 Implant parameterisation

The stem was a straight, collarless, neck-preserving design defined in its own frame and inserted along its axis by pure translation. Its cross-section was a four-quadrant superellipse with a linear keystone term: independent medial and lateral half-widths, independent anterior and posterior half-thicknesses, and (in the 12-variable space) separate squareness exponents and keystone slopes for the anterior and posterior halves, so that the section was asymmetric about both the medio-lateral and the antero-posterior mid-lines. The section shape was kept constant along the stem and scaled with the local area, A(s) = A₀[ρ_tip + (1 − ρ_tip)(1 − s/L)^q], s being the distance from the shoulder; because the sections are nested, translational insertion removes no bone beyond the stem envelope. This zero-overcut property was verified numerically for 65 placements by sweeping the stem surface along the insertion path (4.7 × 10⁶ sampled points, none outside the final stem). The tip was closed by a half-ellipsoidal cap.

The design variables were, in the refocused study (V3): stem length L (20–70 mm), resection level h_cut (25–45 mm from the head centre along the neck axis; 25 mm corresponds to the head–neck junction and 45 mm approximately to the neck base), the insertion angle α between the stem axis and the shaft axis in the neck plane (5–40°), the cortical margin m (−0.25 to 2 mm, see below), the aspect ratio λ (medio-lateral width / antero-posterior thickness, 1.0–2.2), the medial share γ_ml and the anterior share γ_ap of width and thickness (0.35–0.65), the squareness exponent n (2–6) and the keystone slope τ (−0.3 to 0.3). The area-reduction parameters were fixed (ρ_tip = 0.3, q = 1.5) after a global sensitivity analysis on the earlier 11/12-variable space had shown them to have the smallest influence on both outcomes.

The stem size and entry point were not design variables but were set by a templating rule that mimics the intra-operative choice of the largest broach that fits: the shoulder centre was placed on the resection plane with an entry offset e ∈ {−8, …, +8} mm along the medial direction of that plane; for each offset the shoulder area A₀ was found by bisection as the largest value for which every point of the stem surface below the resection plane satisfied d + m ≤ 0.05 mm, d being the signed distance to the endosteal surface; the offset giving the largest A₀ was retained. A negative margin (m = −0.25 mm) therefore allows the stem to bear on the cortex with up to 0.25 mm of interference, whereas m = +1 mm keeps a 1 mm layer of cancellous bone everywhere. Designs were rejected if no size fitted, if the tip area fell below 12 mm², or if A₀ was smaller than the cross-section of the prosthetic neck (π·7² = 154 mm²). In the 9-variable space 50 % of 8192 Sobol samples were feasible.

### 2.3 Finite-element model

For each design the femur solids were truncated 150 mm below the lesser trochanter and cut by the resection plane (normal to the neck axis); the reamed cavity was the stem envelope shrunk radially by the press-fit interference δ = 0.10 mm, and a Boolean fragmentation produced conforming cortical and cancellous volumes. The prosthetic neck (radius 7 mm, from the resection plane to the anatomical head centre) and a 14 mm head were fused to the stem. All parts were meshed with quadratic tetrahedra (Abaqus C3D10, straight-sided) using gmsh, with a target element size of 2.0 mm at the bone–stem interface, 6 mm in the far field, and a Delaunay–HXT–refinement retry sequence when the initial mesh failed. A typical model had 26 000–29 000 elements (about 8 000 cortical, 10 000 cancellous, 8 000 stem). Cortical bone (E = 16.8 GPa), cancellous bone (E = 400 MPa, uniform, as appropriate for a Dorr A metaphysis) and the Ti-6Al-4V implant (E = 110 GPa) were linear-elastic and isotropic with ν = 0.3 [1,2,8].

Two models were solved per design, following common practice [1,2,4]. In the *press-fit/friction* model the bone–stem interface was a surface-to-surface, small-sliding contact pair with hard normal behaviour and Coulomb friction (μ = 0.3); the interference was introduced in a first step (`*CONTACT INTERFERENCE, SHRINK`) with the head held fixed, the head was released in a second step, and the joint load was applied in a third [2]. In the *bonded* model the interface was tied (`*TIE, ADJUST=NO`) and the load applied in a single step; this model provided the bone strain-energy density and the stem stresses. The distal cut face was fully constrained. The hip contact force was distributed equally over all head nodes within 14 mm of the head centre. Its direction was taken from the average stair-climbing peak of Bergmann et al. [6] (20° in the frontal and 18° in the sagittal plane, expressed in the femur-based system of that study and transformed to the model frame) and its magnitude was 2391 N; the antero-posterior component (698 N) produces a torque about the stem axis of roughly 30 N·m, which stair climbing is known to impose on the implant [6]. An intact femur, truncated at the same level and loaded identically, served as the reference for strain-energy density. All analyses were run in Abaqus/Standard 2025 with 4 cores per job.

### 2.4 Outcome measures

*Primary stability* was quantified from the friction model as the tangential slip increment at every interface node between the end of the release step and the peak-load step (the resultant of the two slip components), i.e. the recoverable relative motion under one loading event [4]. Its area-weighted 95th percentile over the interface (f₁, µm) was the stability objective; the area fractions of the interface with slip above 40 µm and 150 µm, the thresholds usually associated with bone ingrowth and fibrous encapsulation [4], were recorded as descriptors, and the fraction of open, slipping and sticking nodes was logged.

*Proximal load transfer* was quantified from the bonded model as the mean strain-energy density (ΣELSE/ΣEVOL) of all bone elements in a Gruen zone 7 region (medial half, x ≥ 0, from the resection plane down to 10 mm below the lesser trochanter), relative to the same region of the intact femur, following the strain-energy formulation of Huiskes et al. [5]: ΔSED = 1 − SED_implanted/SED_intact. Positive values indicate stress shielding, negative values proximal overloading. Gruen zone 1 was recorded in the same way.

*Bone loss* (V3 objective) was purely geometric: the volume of bone between the resection plane and the head–neck junction plane (25 mm from the head centre), counted on the distance-field grid, plus the volume of the stem envelope below the resection plane (reamed cancellous bone).

*Stem stress* was the volume-weighted 99th percentile of the von Mises stress in the stem below the resection plane (bonded model), constrained to ≤ 440 MPa.

### 2.5 Verification

The pipeline was checked in the sense of ASME V&V 40 code and calculation verification, and reported following the checklist of Erdemir et al. [9]. Sign checks were run on one design: removing the antero-posterior load component reduced f₁ (7.34 → 6.52 µm), removing the interference increased it (→ 24.3 µm), raising μ to 0.8 reduced it (→ 3.38 µm), and rebuilding the mesh and re-solving reproduced all outcomes to machine precision. Mesh convergence was assessed on two designs with interface sizes of 2.0, 1.4 and 1.0 mm (grid-convergence index after Celik's procedure) and, because the optimiser only needs correct ranking, by the Spearman rank correlation of f₁ and ΔSED between the 2.0 and 1.4 mm meshes over 12 designs (0.92 and 0.99). The 2.0 mm mesh was retained. The intact reference was evaluated at 2.0 and 1.4 mm (Gruen 7 strain-energy density differing by 1.6 %); the 1.0 mm intact model exceeded the available memory and the 1.4 mm value was used in its place.

### 2.6 Design of experiments and multi-objective Bayesian optimisation

Candidate designs were drawn from scrambled Sobol sequences in the unit hypercube, mapped to physical ranges, templated and filtered by the feasibility rules of §2.2. A reference set of 64 feasible Sobol designs (all with the head–neck-junction resection) was solved in full; it served three purposes: an unbiased picture of the objective space, an independent test set for the surrogate, and a same-budget random-sampling baseline. Global sensitivity was estimated on a Gaussian-process (GP) surrogate fitted to all solved designs, using Jansen's estimator of total-effect Sobol indices on the posterior mean, per variable and per variable group.

Each objective and the constraint were modelled by an independent GP with an ARD Matérn-5/2 kernel (lengthscale prior Γ(3, 6), input normalised to [0, 1], outputs standardised), fitted by maximising the marginal likelihood (GPyTorch 1.15.2, BoTorch 0.18.1). Candidates were selected in batches of q = 4 with the noisy expected hypervolume improvement acquisition function in its log-transformed form (qLogNEHVI [10]), with 128 quasi-Monte-Carlo samples, constraints entered as outcome constraints, and the discrete optimisation carried out over the feasible Sobol pool (8192 samples). The reference point for the hypervolume was set to the worst feasible value of each objective in the warm-start set plus 10 % of its range. In the first campaign (12 variables, objectives f₁ and |ΔSED|), three seeds were started from 24 initial Sobol designs and run for up to 10 rounds; the hypervolume ratio relative to the best-known front was compared with the median of 200 random orderings of the reference set at the same number of evaluations. In the refocused campaign (V3, 9 variables, objectives f₁ and bone loss, constraints |ΔSED| ≤ 30 % and stem stress ≤ 440 MPa combined into one normalised maximum violation), all previously solved designs (162, of which 126 satisfied the constraints) were mapped into the new space (h_cut = 25 mm; n and τ as the mean of the anterior and posterior values) and used as a warm start, and the optimiser was run for 10 rounds per seed without a new initial design. The approach of using a small number of high-fidelity FE runs to train a surrogate for short-stem design follows Cilla et al. [7]; the FE micromotion and length-dependence benchmarks were taken from Reimeringer et al. [1] and Kanaizumi et al. [8], and the in-vitro strain and stability behaviour of short stems from Bieger et al. [3].

### 2.7 Computational workflow and reporting

Pre-processing (templating, CAD, meshing, input-file generation), post-processing and the surrogate/acquisition steps ran on a Linux workstation (Intel i5-12400F, 12 threads), and only the Abaqus solves ran on a Windows workstation (AMD Ryzen 7 9700X, two concurrent jobs of 4 cores). Jobs were exchanged through a file-based queue; every design was identified by a hash of its specification, the mesh size and the source-code version, so that repeated requests returned cached results and every figure in the report could be regenerated from its data file. Failures were classified (L1 infeasible geometry, L3 meshing, L4 solver, L5 export) and reported. Solve times were 4–6 min for the friction model and about 40 s for the bonded model at 2.0 mm. All figures, tables and acceptance criteria were registered in a machine-readable specification and generated with data-driven titles.
